Project Overview

Three-phase framework: Input (Bait) → Process (Psychology) → Output (Defense)

Input — The Bait

Delivery methods, phishing types (mass, spear, whale), and technical disguises like domain spoofing.

Process — Psychology

Cognitive triggers that bypass logic: Authority, Urgency, Curiosity, and Fear/Greed exploitation.

Output — Defense

Triage checklist, decision tree, and Pause → Verify → Report protocol for all suspicious messages.

Security Thinking

Applying the human firewall mentality — every user is a potential attack surface and a potential defender.

Incident Triage

Classifying threats as Safe → Close, Suspicious → Warn, or Malicious → Block & Escalate with documented reasoning.

1
PAUSE
Recognize the cognitive trigger (Urgency, Fear, Authority) and stop interacting. Apply the Five-Minute Rule.
2
VERIFY
Confirm through a secondary, out-of-band channel. Email came in? Verify via phone to a known number.
3
REPORT
Use the internal reporting plugin. Never just delete — reporting lets the security team purge it from all inboxes.

Phishing Triage Checklist

A non-expert guide for every employee — click each item as you inspect the suspicious message.

Inspection progress
0 / 11
1. Check the sender's actual email address
Expand the "From" field. Does the display name match the actual domain? E.g. "Microsoft Support <hacker@gmail.com>" is a red flag.
Critical
2. Inspect links before clicking (hover or long-press)
Read the URL from right-to-left to find the true root domain. Watch for typosquatting (amaz0n.com), homoglyphs, and subdomain traps.
Critical
3. Identify the cognitive trigger being used
Is there urgency ("Act in 30 minutes"), authority ("Your CEO demands"), curiosity ("See what was said about you"), or fear/greed?
Critical
4. Check for requests to bypass normal procedures
"Do not discuss with anyone." "Bypass standard procedure." "Process immediately." These are hallmarks of BEC and urgent fraud attacks.
Critical
5. Scan for suspicious or dangerous attachments
Uncommon extensions like .iso, .js, .scr, .hta, or HTML files posing as normal documents can run malicious code when opened.
High
6. Look for unexpected requests for sensitive info
Legitimate organizations never ask for passwords, MFA codes, or payment details via email. Any such request is a major red flag.
High
7. Watch for MFA fatigue attacks
Multiple unsolicited authenticator push notifications designed to wear you down. Never approve a login you didn't initiate.
High
8. Check for fake forwarded email chains (FW:)
Pasted headers with odd timestamps from conversations you were never part of — used to build false credibility and context.
High
9. Evaluate contextual integrity
Does this request make sense given who sent it and when? Would your CEO normally text you about a wire transfer from an airport?
Medium
10. Be alert to unsolicited QR codes
QR codes bypass desktop URL filters. Malicious codes in PDFs or on physical posters can direct you to spoofed login pages on mobile.
Medium
11. Check for Browser-in-the-Browser (BitB) pop-ups
Fake SSO login windows that cannot be dragged outside the main browser. If a "popup" won't move out of the browser window, it's fake.
Medium

After Completing the Checklist

0–2 Red Flags → SAFE
Close the ticket. No action required. Log for reporting metrics.
3–5 Red Flags → SUSPICIOUS
Warn the user. Advise them not to click anything. Escalate to IT.
6+ Red Flags → MALICIOUS
Block domain immediately. Escalate. Report via internal plugin.

Phishing Attack Taxonomy

Understanding all attack vectors — phishing goes far beyond the email inbox.

By Targeting Level

Mass Phishing Mass
Generic lures mimicking ubiquitous brands like Amazon or PayPal. High volume, low personalization, roughly 1% engagement rate. The attacker casts a wide net hoping statistical chance yields victims.
Generic greeting ("Dear Customer") Common brand impersonation Urgency language Suspicious link
Spear Phishing Targeted
Contextual precision using OSINT (like LinkedIn) to reference specific project names, colleagues, or internal jargon. Makes the email feel highly credible and personally relevant to the victim.
Uses victim's real name References real colleagues Internal jargon OSINT-derived context
Whaling (BEC) Executive
Targeting the C-Suite. Focuses on founders and executives to execute high-value wire transfers (BEC) or acquire M&A documents. The Quanta Computer exploit stole $100M from Google & Facebook this way.
CEO impersonation Wire transfer requests High secrecy demands Domain spoofing

By Attack Channel

Smishing (SMS)
Malicious links via text, often disguised as package deliveries or urgent bank alerts. Mobile users are less likely to inspect URLs carefully.
Vishing (Voice)
Live calls using caller ID spoofing to impersonate IT support or government agencies. Creates real-time social pressure.
Quishing (QR Codes)
Malicious QR codes placed on physical posters or in digital PDFs, forcing users onto mobile devices where spoofed domains are harder to spot.
Search Engine Phishing
SEO poisoning that manipulates search results to place malicious lookalike websites above legitimate login portals.
TOAD / Callback Scams
Emails containing no malicious links — only a phone number urging you to call "Support" to resolve a fake subscription charge.
Deepfake Live-Meeting Fraud
AI audio/video impersonation of an executive during an ad-hoc meeting, demanding mid-call vendor payment changes.

Complete Red Flag Reference

All 11 red flags from the DecodeLabs training kit — grouped by category for quick reference.

Technical Red Flags (Email Header & Links)

RF-1: Sender-Domain Mismatch

The display name conflicts with the actual routing domain. E.g. "Microsoft Support" sending from hacker@gmail.com. Always expand the From field.

RF-2: Suspicious / Spoofed URLs

Typosquatting (amaz0n.com), homoglyph attacks (Cyrillic chars), combosquatting (yourcompany-secure-login.com). Always read URLs right-to-left.

Cognitive & Psychological Triggers

Authority
Impersonating the C-suite, IT department, or law enforcement to demand unquestioned compliance. Bypasses critical thinking through social hierarchy.
"This is your CEO. Process this wire transfer immediately and do not speak to anyone about it."
Urgency
Creating artificial time pressure to trigger a fight-or-flight response, reducing rational processing and forcing hasty decisions.
"Your account will be locked in 30 minutes unless you verify now. Act immediately."
Curiosity
Exploiting the human need to fill knowledge gaps. Creates an irresistible pull to find out what "they" said or what secret document awaits.
"See what your colleague said about you in this shared document [link]"
Fear / Greed
Threatening negative consequences (legal action, account closure, financial penalty) or promising unearned rewards (prize winners, refunds).
"You have been selected for a $1,000 gift card. Claim now — expires in 1 hour."

Contextual & Modern Red Flags (RF 5–11)

RF-5: Urgent Bypass Requests

Demands for secrecy or explicit instructions to bypass normal procurement/security procedures.

RF-6: Requests for Sensitive Info

Unexpected prompts for MFA codes, passwords, or payment detail changes over email.

RF-7: Activity Alerts

Alarmist unusual sign-in warnings that point directly to a login page instead of advising manual navigation.

RF-8: MFA Fatigue

Multiple, unprompted authenticator push notifications designed to wear the user down until they hit Approve.

RF-9: Security Callback Scams (TOAD)

Emails with only a phone number urging you to call Support about a fake subscription charge — no malicious link.

RF-10: QR Code Prompts

Unsolicited QR codes demanding a scan to secure your account, bypassing desktop URL filters entirely.

RF-11: Deepfake Live-Meeting Fraud

Audio or video impersonation of an executive during an ad-hoc meeting, demanding mid-call vendor payment changes. Verify independently before any action.
